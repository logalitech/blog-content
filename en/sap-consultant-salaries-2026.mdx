Search for "SAP consultant salary" and you will find three different answers on the first page, each one sure of itself. One says it is a mediocre career. Another says you make a fortune. Neither tells you which SAP consultant it is talking about, and that is exactly the piece of information that decides the answer.

## Why the figure you see is useless

An SAP consultant is not a single profession, it is half a dozen packed into one label. Someone doing level two functional support at a large consultancy, on payroll, with no room to negotiate, does not earn the same as someone billing by project as a freelancer with ten years of RAP and BTP behind them. Both show up in the same search for "SAP consultant salary", and the figures they quote cannot be averaged without the result lying to you.

On top of that, almost none of the figures in circulation has a source. It is a number someone once wrote in a forum, which someone else cited without verifying, and which now appears in ten articles as if it were official data. There is no single, public, up to date SAP salary survey covering every country and every profile, and any article that gives you a single global figure is oversimplifying something that does not lend itself to simplification.

What you can do, and what is actually worth doing, is look at which variables really move that number, so you know where to play your hand.

## The variables that really change the number

**Technical specialization, not years of experience.** The market does not pay the same for someone who maintains standard modules as for someone who builds clean extensions in ABAP Cloud on released APIs, or someone who administers BTP at the level of subaccounts, entitlements and connectivity. These are scarce skills because most consultants with years of experience were trained in classic ABAP, and migrating that way of thinking costs more than learning it from scratch. Ten years of reports are not worth the same as three years of well-executed ABAP Cloud.

**Self-employed or on payroll.** A freelancer who bills by project takes on the risk of not having a permanent contract, and that risk is paid for: the hourly or daily rate is usually higher than the payroll equivalent for the same skill level. In exchange, there are no paid vacations, no guaranteed sick leave, and no fixed salary in months without a project. It is not an objectively better option, it is a change in the risk structure that your salary reflects.

**The country you work from, not the client's country.** With remote projects, it is increasingly common to work for a client in Germany or the United States from a country with a much lower cost of living. That radically changes what that salary means in practice, even if the gross figure in the offer is identical to that of someone who lives where the client is.

## What this looks like in practice

Three real profiles to make the above tangible, without inventing a figure that cannot be verified.

**The payroll functional consultant who has not yet migrated to ABAP Cloud.** Ten years with the same client, good business knowledge, but standard modules and no S/4HANA project on the resume yet. Their salary moves little, not because they lack experience, but because the market does not pay for seniority itself, it pays for scarcity, and that profile is not scarce: there are many consultants with the same track record competing for the same position.

**The junior developer with six months of RAP and released APIs, working remotely for a client in another country.** Fewer years than the previous one, but they fit exactly into the gap where there is demand and little supply. They earn above what their seniority would predict, because the market does not compare resumes, it compares what problem each one solves and how many can solve it today.

**The freelancer with BTP and ABAP Cloud who bills per project for two European consultancies at the same time.** No payroll, no paid vacation, with months of more work and months of less. Their hourly rate is higher than that of an employee with the same skill, but that gross figure is not directly comparable to a fixed salary: it absorbs the risk that the freelancer takes on and that the payroll employee does not.

All three profiles can appear in the same search for "SAP consultant salary". No single figure represents all three at once, and that is why the right question is never "how much do you earn", it is "which of these three am I, or which one do I want to be".

## Where to start if you come from outside

If you come from another technology and want to enter through the gap where there is real demand, the order matters more than the number of things you learn.

First, ABAP Cloud and the extensibility model, not classic ABAP. It is the part that almost nobody has mastered yet and the one that sets your profile apart from someone who has spent twenty years writing reports. [ABAP Cloud is not Cobol](/blog/abap-cloud-no-es-cobol/) is the starting point if you program.

Second, if your role is more functional than technical, the ability to tell what is viable from what is not is worth as much as knowing how to write the code: [what a functional consultant should understand about ABAP Cloud](/blog/consultor-funcional-abap-cloud/) covers exactly that.

Third, decide on your risk structure with a clear head, not just your specialization. Permanent employment or freelancing is not a technical question, it is a question about how much variability you can take on this year. Nobody can answer it for you, but at least now you know which variable you are deciding.
